## 2. Research Methods (~100 words)

**Answer:** I will approach this question in three phases, plus a literature review covering NeuralFoil, XFOIL, transonic drag rise, and the historical NACA wind-tunnel program. In Phase A, I will compare NeuralFoil's predictions in Python against published wind-tunnel measurements at matched Mach number, Reynolds number, and transition conditions, producing a validity map that shows where the tool is accurate and where it is not. In Phase B, I will keep the neural network frozen and recalibrate the simple drag-rise formula applied after it, fitting its constants to experimental data and validating on airfoils excluded from the fit. In Phase C, which reuses Phase A's comparisons and needs no new data, I will test whether the tool's built-in confidence score is calibrated against its real errors and convert it into a usable bound of the form "with ninety percent coverage, the drag error is within a stated number of counts."

## 3. Academic Context (~400 words)

**Answer:** Researchers have built machine-learning tools that predict airfoil performance thousands of times faster than traditional methods, but these tools have mainly been tested against the software they learned from, not against real experiments. My project sits at the meeting point of three bodies of work: the NeuralFoil tool itself, the historical NACA wind-tunnel program that produced systematic airfoil measurements, and recent work on improving machine-learning aerodynamic models.

Key papers and reports:

- **arxiv.org/abs/2503.16323 (Sharpe & Hansman, 2025).** This paper introduces NeuralFoil, a physics-informed machine-learning tool trained on nearly eight million XFOIL data points. It is the paper whose accuracy claims my project tests. The authors state that predictions of when transonic flow occurs are "reasonably trustworthy," but that wave drag beyond that point is not.
- **ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19930084305 (NACA TN 3607, Daley & Dick, 1956).** This report measured lift and drag on NACA airfoil families with systematically varied thickness and camber at Mach numbers up to 1.0. It provides exactly the controlled experimental drag-rise data my recalibration will be fitted to.
- **ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19880002254 (NASA TM-100019, McCroskey, 1987).** This report compared NACA 0012 measurements from more than forty wind tunnels and quantified how much they disagree. It matters because experimental scatter sets the "noise floor": no model can be validated more precisely than experiments agree with each other.
- **arxiv.org/abs/2512.10287 (Sarker, Batley, Sarojini, & Saha, 2025).** This paper presents KHRONOS, a multi-fidelity surrogate that blends sparse high-fidelity CFD data with low-fidelity predictions generated by NeuralFoil. It is the closest recent work involving NeuralFoil, and it differs from mine in two ways: it improves accuracy by combining fidelities rather than by correcting NeuralFoil's own formulas, and it uses simulation rather than experiment as its reference.

My project builds on this background by producing the first systematic comparison of NeuralFoil against experimental data across flow regimes and airfoil families, and then using that comparison to improve the tool's weakest layer.

## 4. How Your Research Fits (~200 words)

**Answer:** This research adds three things that do not yet exist in the literature. First, a wind-tunnel-based validity map of NeuralFoil, since previous testing compared it mainly to XFOIL, the tool it learned from, which cannot reveal mistakes the two share. Second, the first published check of whether NeuralFoil's confidence score actually predicts its error against experiment, extended into calibrated error bounds with measured coverage, so the score becomes something a user can act on. Third, an experimental recalibration of its transonic drag-rise formula. The tool's own author retuned that formula against CFD solutions, and the KHRONOS project blended NeuralFoil with CFD data in a multi-fidelity model, but no one has calibrated the formula to real wind-tunnel measurements with held-out airfoils. My claim is deliberately precise: the first calibration against experimental data with quantified held-out error, not the first correction of NeuralFoil. A released, machine-readable dataset of the digitized measurements supports all three; the paper counts it as a fourth, supporting contribution.

The literature review is structured in six parts: (1) airfoil geometry and the NACA families; (2) how CL, CD, and their ratio measure performance; (3) transonic drag rise and the critical Mach number; (4) NeuralFoil and machine-learning aerodynamic tools; (5) the historical wind-tunnel record as ground truth, including why experiments themselves have error bars; and (6) how surrogate models have been validated and improved before, and the gap this study fills.

## 5. Roadblocks and Concerns (~150 words)

**Answer:** The biggest practical roadblock is data extraction, because most 1950s NACA measurements are plotted curves in scanned PDFs rather than tables. I will cap digitization at a mentor-approved list of about 35 to 40 curves, extract the two cleanest-format reports first (Harris and Ferri) so a minimum dataset always exists, and double-digitize a sample of points to measure my own extraction error. A second risk is silently comparing the wrong quantities, since old reports mix lift and normal-force coefficients and different transition states. Each source therefore gets a written conventions check before use, plus one sanity-check point that must match NeuralFoil's subsonic prediction before I trust the rest. A third risk is that my recalibration fails to beat the original formula by more than experimental scatter. The design protects against this, because Phase A is frozen as a complete, submittable paper before any fitting starts. Even a negative Phase B result, meaning the simple formula cannot be improved with this data, is a real and reportable finding.

## 6. List of 10 sources

1. **arxiv.org/abs/2503.16323.** Introduces NeuralFoil, a physics-informed machine-learning airfoil tool trained on nearly eight million XFOIL data points. States its own transonic limitation: drag-rise onset is trustworthy, wave-drag magnitude is not. This is the tool under study.
2. **web.mit.edu/drela/Public/web/xfoil/.** XFOIL, the classical fast airfoil tool combining a panel method with a boundary-layer model. NeuralFoil is trained entirely on its output, so XFOIL's subsonic validity is the ceiling NeuralFoil inherits. Explains why independent experimental validation is needed.
3. **ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19930084305 (NACA TN 3607).** Systematic wind-tunnel tests of NACA airfoils with varied thickness and camber up to Mach 1.0, including drag-versus-Mach curves. Primary calibration dataset for the recalibrated drag-rise formula.
4. **ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19810014503 (NASA TM-81927, Harris).** NACA 0012 force data at Mach 0.30 to 0.86 in the Langley 8-Foot Transonic Pressure Tunnel, published as plotted figures on fine grids in the NTRS scan. The cleanest transonic dataset available, used as an independent-facility check and excluded from all fitting.
5. **ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19880002254 (NASA TM-100019, McCroskey).** Meta-analysis of NACA 0012 data from more than forty wind tunnels. Defines the experimental scatter band used as this study's noise floor and grades which historical datasets to trust.
6. **ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19930082349 (NACA TN 1546).** Force data for twenty-four NACA 16-series airfoils at Mach 0.3 to 0.8. Because the 16-series is a different family from the calibration set, it serves as this study's family holdout.
7. **ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19930092764 (NACA WR L-143, Ferri).** High-speed force data for twenty-four airfoils at Mach 0.40 to 0.94, presented as per-airfoil plotted figures in the NTRS scan. A multi-airfoil calibration set digitized under the same protocol as the other sources; the report itself notes that thickness dominates high-Mach behavior.
8. **ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19890008197 (NASA TP-2890).** Supercritical airfoil SC(2)-0714 tested at Mach 0.60 to 0.76 across a wide Reynolds range. Its design philosophy is absent from the calibration families, which makes it the pre-registered boundary-of-validity test where transfer is *not* expected.
9. **ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19930090976 (NACA TR-824).** The classic summary of airfoil data, with low-speed section characteristics and theory-based critical-Mach charts. Anchors the subsonic validation block; its critical-Mach charts serve as a theory consistency check, not as experimental truth.
10. **arxiv.org/abs/2512.10287 (Sarker et al., KHRONOS).** A multi-fidelity kernel surrogate that blends high-fidelity CFD with low-fidelity NeuralFoil predictions for flow-field prediction. The closest recent work using NeuralFoil, cited to position this study's narrower, experiment-based contribution.
